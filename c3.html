Trustworthy Large Language Models in Financial Advisory: Opportunities, Challenges, and Future Directions

Opportunities, Evidence, Risks, and a Framework for Trustworthy AI

Dr. Nitin Mishra1, Tamanna Sharma2
1Professor, Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Parul University, Vadodara, India | 2Assistant Professor, Faculty of Management, Parul University, Vadodara, India
Corresponding author: drnitinmishra10@gmail.com  |  Second author: tannusharma2401@gmail.com

Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are moving financial artificial intelligence beyond narrowly defined prediction and classification tasks toward natural-language interaction, document understanding, contextual analysis, and decision support. This paper examines how LLMs can be integrated into financial advisory while preserving the reliability, suitability, transparency, and accountability expected in a high-stakes domain. A structured literature review synthesises research on financial language models, robo-advisory, financial question answering, portfolio selection, retrieval-augmented generation, explainable artificial intelligence, and emerging regulatory and systemic-risk perspectives. The evidence indicates that LLMs can add substantial value in investor dialogue, financial-document interpretation, market-news analysis, portfolio explanation, financial education, and advisor copilot functions. Recent empirical work also suggests that some models can improve diversification or extract economically relevant signals from news. At the same time, results are sensitive to model choice, prompt design, information horizon, and evaluation setting; other studies report failure to consistently outperform simple benchmarks. The central implication is that an LLM should not be treated as the financial decision engine. Instead, it should operate within a layered architecture in which trusted financial data, retrieval, deterministic suitability and risk controls, model validation, and human oversight constrain and verify generated outputs. The paper proposes such a framework and identifies research priorities around grounded generation, numerical reasoning, fairness, privacy, monitoring, benchmark design, and human-AI collaboration.

Keywords: Large Language Models; Financial Advisory; FinTech; Generative AI; Robo-Advisory; Retrieval-Augmented Generation; Explainable AI; Trustworthy AI; Investment Recommendation; Human-AI Collaboration.

1. Introduction

Financial advisory is an unusually demanding application for artificial intelligence because recommendations can directly affect wealth, retirement security, risk exposure, and access to financial products. The traditional advisory relationship combines quantitative analysis with contextual judgement: a client may have a measurable risk tolerance but also tax constraints, liquidity needs, family obligations, behavioural biases, and preferences that are not captured by a single numerical score. Digital advisory platforms attempted to standardise this process through questionnaires and rules-based portfolio construction. Research on robo-advisors shows both their accessibility and their potential to substitute for some forms of human advice, while also raising concerns about algorithmic opacity, limited disclosure, and the quality of client information (Brenner & Meyll, 2020; Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021; Tan, 2020). (Davies, 2020; FSB, 2024).

LLMs change the interaction layer of this ecosystem. Transformer-based models made it possible to represent context at scale (Vaswani et al., 2017), while BERT and subsequent domain-adapted financial language models demonstrated the value of specialised representations for financial text (Devlin et al., 2019; Araci, 2019; Shah et al., 2022). The emergence of generative models adds a different capability: a single system can explain a portfolio, summarise filings, answer a client's question, compare financial products, translate technical language into plain language, and maintain a conversational interface. Finance-specific efforts such as BloombergGPT and FinGPT further demonstrate the movement from general-purpose LLMs toward financial-domain models (Wu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023).

The critical issue, however, is not whether an LLM can produce plausible financial language. It is whether a financial advisory system can use that capability without confusing linguistic fluency with factual reliability, numerical correctness, suitability, or fiduciary responsibility. Financial LLM research highlights hallucination, privacy, efficiency, and evaluation challenges (Lee et al., 2024). Financial question-answering benchmarks such as FinQA and TAT-QA show why the domain is difficult: financial reasoning frequently requires multi-step numerical calculations over tables and text, and earlier language models remained substantially behind expert human performance on such tasks (Chen et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2021).

This paper therefore takes a deliberately conservative position. LLMs have meaningful potential in financial advisory, but their strongest role is as a language and reasoning-support layer within a controlled decision architecture. The contribution is a conceptual framework that separates generated language from validated financial decision logic and integrates retrieval, quantitative models, suitability rules, explainability, security, monitoring, and human oversight.

2. Research Questions

RQ1: Which financial-advisory tasks can benefit most from LLM-based natural-language and contextual capabilities?

RQ2: What evidence exists that LLMs can support investment and portfolio decisions, and where is the evidence inconsistent?

RQ3: What architecture is required to make LLM-enabled financial advice grounded, suitable, explainable, secure, and auditable?

RQ4: What research and governance mechanisms are needed before LLMs can be trusted in higher-stakes advisory settings?

3. Methodology

This study uses a structured conceptual literature-review approach. Literature was identified through targeted searches of peer-reviewed and reputable research sources covering LLMs, financial NLP, portfolio decision-making, robo-advisory, retrieval-augmented generation, explainability, and AI governance. Priority was given to peer-reviewed journal and conference publications and to authoritative policy sources where the issue was regulatory or systemic rather than empirical. The synthesis follows four themes: capability, evidence, risk, and governance. Studies reporting positive performance are not treated as proof of general superiority; they are compared with work reporting benchmark failures or deployment constraints. No primary experiment, survey, or bibliometric study was conducted, and the framework proposed in this paper is conceptual rather than experimentally validated.

The evolution should not be interpreted as a simple replacement sequence. The strongest architecture is likely to combine generations. A financial institution may retain deterministic portfolio and suitability rules while using an LLM to gather information conversationally and explain the result. This distinction is consistent with the broader AI-in-finance literature: AI can support operational efficiency, regulatory compliance, analytics, and product customisation, but model risk, data quality, concentration, cyber risk, and governance remain material concerns (Financial Stability Board [FSB], 2024). (Tao et al., 2021).

The LLM layer is therefore best understood as an adaptive interface over a heterogeneous financial system. It can translate a client's natural-language question into structured tasks, retrieve relevant evidence, call a quantitative model, and turn validated outputs into a comprehensible explanation. That is different from asking the LLM to invent the portfolio itself. (Du et al., 2025).

5. Evidence on LLMs in Financial Decision-Making

Evidence is increasingly positive but far from uniform. Oehler and Horn (2024) compared ChatGPT with 17 robo-advisors across three investor profiles and reported that ChatGPT recommendations aligned with the profiles and a benchmark derived from academic literature. Another study found that ChatGPT-based asset selection produced statistically stronger diversification than randomly selected assets, suggesting a potential copilot role for portfolio managers (2024). More recent work using different ChatGPT models reported that model choice mattered and that models could adjust portfolio characteristics to different risk appetites (2025).

However, favourable results should not be generalised to unconditional investment superiority. A 2025 experiment evaluating Gemini 1.5 Flash with anonymised stock data found that it did not consistently beat an equal-weighted portfolio or the S&P 500 and that risk-adjusted performance deteriorated over longer horizons. The implication is important: an LLM can demonstrate useful decision-support capability in one evaluation design while failing to deliver persistent outperformance in another.

Text-based market analysis provides another example. Lopez-Lira and Tang's work finds that GPT-4 can extract economically relevant information from news and that more capable models perform better at interpreting certain market reactions. The later journal version reports evidence consistent with stronger information processing and changing return predictability as LLM adoption rises. Such findings are valuable, but they address a narrower problem—interpreting news and market responses—than the full suitability problem faced by a financial advisor.

Financial-language research reinforces the same point. BloombergGPT demonstrates that large domain-specific training can improve financial tasks without sacrificing general capability, while FinGPT emphasises a data-centric and open-source route to financial LLM development (Wu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). The implication for advisory systems is that domain adaptation matters, but domain adaptation alone does not solve governance. A model can be financially knowledgeable and still hallucinate, expose sensitive information, or generate an unsuitable recommendation. (Du et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023).

6. High-Value Applications in Financial Advisory

6.1 Investor profiling and conversational onboarding

LLMs can convert unstructured client dialogue into candidate structured attributes such as goals, time horizon, liquidity constraints, risk preferences, and investment experience. This can reduce the friction associated with rigid questionnaires. Yet inferred attributes should be treated as hypotheses requiring confirmation. A conversational system must distinguish between what the client explicitly stated, what the system inferred, and what remains unknown.

6.2 Financial-document interpretation

Financial reports combine narrative, tables, footnotes, accounting terminology, and numerical relationships. FinQA and TAT-QA demonstrate that the challenge is not simply reading text; it includes selecting supporting evidence and performing numerical reasoning over heterogeneous representations (Chen et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2021). LLMs can make such information more accessible, but numerical calculations should be delegated to validated tools whenever precision matters.

6.3 Market and news interpretation

LLMs are particularly useful when the problem is semantic synthesis rather than direct arithmetic. They can summarise a set of announcements, identify competing interpretations, and explain how a new event could affect an existing investment thesis. The system should ground such explanations in dated sources because financial information changes rapidly.

6.4 Portfolio explanation and education

One of the most defensible uses of an LLM is to explain an already validated portfolio. It can translate volatility, diversification, drawdown, fees, and asset allocation into client-specific language. Explainable-AI research in finance shows that transparency and traceability are central because stakeholders must understand why a model-assisted decision was made (Weber et al., 2024). Explanation, however, should be evidence-linked and should not be treated as proof that the underlying recommendation is correct.

6.5 Advisor copilot

A hybrid copilot can prepare meeting briefs, summarise client interactions, surface changes in portfolio risk, draft explanations, and retrieve product or regulatory information. This role preserves an explicit human decision point and is therefore more compatible with the accountability requirements of advisory services than a fully autonomous chatbot.

Principle 1 — Separate language generation from financial decision logic. The LLM should not be the sole source of truth for prices, returns, product terms, calculations, or suitability. It should request current data from trusted sources and pass quantitative tasks to validated tools.

Principle 2 — Ground every material claim. Retrieval-augmented generation can reduce reliance on stale parametric knowledge by bringing relevant external evidence into the generation process. Self-RAG further illustrates how retrieval and self-reflection can improve factuality and citation behaviour (Lewis et al., 2020; Asai et al., 2024). In an advisory system, retrieved evidence should include timestamp, source, version, and provenance.

Principle 3 — Enforce deterministic suitability controls. A recommendation should pass explicit rules for risk capacity, concentration, liquidity, product eligibility, investment horizon, and other constraints. These controls should be independent of the LLM's prose.

Principle 4 — Make uncertainty visible. The system should distinguish facts, calculations, forecasts, assumptions, and interpretations. An answer should be able to say that evidence is incomplete rather than filling the gap with plausible language.

Principle 5 — Preserve human accountability. Human oversight is essential when the recommendation is material, ambiguous, outside model limits, or sensitive to missing client information. The SEC's robo-adviser guidance emphasises disclosure and reasonable suitability determination; the same logic becomes more—not less—important when conversational AI can generate highly persuasive explanations.

These risks align with current governance perspectives. NIST identifies validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness as interdependent characteristics of trustworthy AI (NIST, 2023). The FSB similarly highlights third-party dependencies, market correlations, cyber risk, model risk, data quality, governance, fraud, and disinformation as important AI-related financial-sector vulnerabilities (FSB, 2024). These concerns become more relevant as LLM systems gain access to financial data, external tools, and automated workflows.

9. Regulatory and Ethical Context

Financial advice is governed by principles that existed before generative AI, including suitability, disclosure, fiduciary responsibility, recordkeeping, and consumer protection. Generative systems do not remove these obligations merely because the interaction is conversational. Instead, they create new questions about who approved an output, which information was used, how the model was tested, and whether a generated explanation accurately reflects the decision process.

The European Union's AI Act provides a useful reference point for risk-based governance. It classifies certain AI uses for evaluating the creditworthiness of natural persons as high-risk, reflecting the potential effect on access to financial resources and the risk of discriminatory outcomes. While not every financial advisory use falls into the same category, the regulatory direction is clear: applications with significant effects require stronger controls, documentation, risk management, and oversight. (European Union, 2024).

From a systemic perspective, the issue extends beyond individual clients. Widespread reliance on similar models, vendors, or datasets can create correlated behaviour and concentration risk. The FSB's 2025 monitoring work emphasises the importance of tracking adoption, third-party dependencies, and emerging vulnerabilities rather than waiting for failures to become systemic.

10. Future Research Agenda

10.1 Evaluation beyond accuracy

Future studies should evaluate advisory LLMs using multi-dimensional metrics: factuality, numerical accuracy, suitability, calibration, fairness, explanation quality, robustness, and client comprehension. Financial benchmarks should include realistic constraints rather than isolated question-answering tasks. (Weber et al., 2024).

10.2 Grounded and tool-using systems

Research should compare standalone LLMs with systems that use RAG, financial databases, knowledge graphs, calculators, optimisation engines, and policy rules. The key research question is not merely whether retrieval improves answer quality, but whether it reduces consequential advisory errors.

10.3 Human-AI collaboration

Advisor-in-the-loop studies should test when humans detect model failures, when AI explanations improve decision quality, and when automation instead creates overreliance. Experimental designs should measure both financial outcomes and cognitive outcomes such as appropriate scepticism.

10.4 Fairness and personalisation

Personalisation can improve suitability but can also amplify hidden proxies for protected characteristics. Future datasets should support subgroup evaluation while respecting privacy. Fairness should be evaluated at the recommendation level rather than only at model-output level. (Winder et al., 2025).

10.5 Regulatory-compliant agentic finance

As LLM agents acquire the ability to call tools, retrieve records, rebalance portfolios, or execute workflows, research must address permissions, transaction limits, dual controls, rollback, auditability, and incident response. Agent autonomy should expand only as verification and governance become stronger. (FSB, 2025).

11. Discussion

The literature supports a nuanced conclusion. LLMs are already useful for tasks that benefit from language, synthesis, and contextual interpretation. The evidence is especially persuasive for financial-document understanding, question answering, market-news interpretation, explanation, and conversational assistance. Evidence for autonomous investment performance is more variable. Some experiments report encouraging portfolio or signal results, while other studies fail to demonstrate persistent superiority to simple benchmarks. Consequently, claims that LLMs can replace financial advisors or consistently beat the market are not supported by the current evidence base.

The distinction between advice generation and decision validation is therefore central. A useful advisory system can let an LLM interpret a client's goals, retrieve relevant information, explain a portfolio, and present alternatives. It should then rely on trusted financial data and validated quantitative or rule-based components to determine whether a recommendation satisfies hard constraints. The resulting decision should be accompanied by evidence and uncertainty and, where appropriate, reviewed by a human advisor.

This architecture also changes how explainability should be understood. A fluent explanation generated after a decision is not necessarily a faithful explanation of why the decision occurred. Explainability should instead be anchored to the actual evidence, model outputs, calculations, and constraints that produced the recommendation. This is consistent with the finance XAI literature, which emphasises traceability and transparency for high-stakes decisions (Weber et al., 2024).

12. Limitations

This study is conceptual and literature based. It does not introduce a new dataset, benchmark, field experiment, or production implementation. The empirical evidence reviewed also varies substantially in task design, model version, market period, data availability, and evaluation methodology. Results obtained with one model or prompting strategy should therefore not be assumed to generalise to all LLMs. Finally, regulatory requirements vary across jurisdictions and continue to evolve, so the governance recommendations should be interpreted as architectural principles rather than legal advice.

13. Conclusion

LLMs are likely to become an important component of financial advisory, but their most defensible contribution is not autonomous portfolio selection. Their distinctive value lies in making complex financial information easier to retrieve, interpret, discuss, and explain. When paired with trusted data, retrieval, numerical tools, deterministic suitability controls, monitoring, and human oversight, LLMs can extend the reach and usability of financial advisory without requiring the financial decision process to depend on unconstrained text generation.

The future of trustworthy financial advisory is therefore better described as human-AI collaboration than human replacement. The strategic objective is to combine the strengths of language models—speed, synthesis, interaction, and contextual explanation—with the strengths of financial models and human professionals—precision, accountability, domain judgement, and responsibility. The resulting system should optimise not for persuasive answers, but for evidence-grounded, suitable, explainable, secure, and auditable decisions.

Contribution in paper

  • Research gap: The paper moves beyond generic “LLMs in finance” discussion by separating language generation from validated financial decision logic.
  • Contribution: A layered trustworthy-advisory architecture integrates investor context, financial data, LLM/RAG, decision controls, explanation, and human governance.
  • Evidence balance: Positive portfolio and market-signal findings are explicitly contrasted with benchmark failures and model sensitivity.
  • Novelty claim: The framework is conceptual; it should not be presented as experimentally validated until implemented and evaluated.
  • Key limitation: The reviewed literature uses heterogeneous models, prompts, periods, and tasks, limiting direct comparison.
  • Recommended next study: Empirically benchmark a grounded LLM advisory prototype against robo-advisors and human advisors using suitability, factuality, numerical accuracy, and user-understanding metrics.
