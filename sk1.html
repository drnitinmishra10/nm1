Weather-Adaptive Deep Learning for Multi-Class Quadruped Animal Detection

A Critical Review of Robust Detection, Domain Adaptation, and Real-World Deployment
Dr. Nitin Mishra
Professor, Computer Science & Engineering, Parul University
drnitinmishra10@gmail.com
Abstract
Deep-learning-based object detection has become an important tool for wildlife monitoring, livestock management, ecological conservation, road-safety systems, and automated surveillance. Yet a detector trained and evaluated primarily under favorable visual conditions can lose reliability when rain, fog, haze, snow, dust, low illumination, seasonal change, camera variation, or background shifts alter the visual distribution of the scene. This review examines the literature at the intersection of multi-class quadruped animal detection, deep learning, adverse-weather robustness, domain adaptation, domain generalization, and deployment-oriented computer vision. A broad scholarly search was conducted using combinations of animal-detection, wildlife, livestock, YOLO, object-detection, adverse-weather, domain-adaptation, and robustness terms. When the candidate literature exceeded 50 papers, the review prioritized peer-reviewed studies, substantive reviews, animal-detection experiments, environmental/domain-variation studies, reproducible evaluations, and recent methodological contributions. The synthesis shows that YOLO-family detectors, CNN-based architectures, lightweight models, and increasingly transformer-based approaches dominate animal-detection research. However, the literature remains much stronger on model improvement within a particular dataset than on controlled cross-weather and cross-domain validation. Existing animal reviews identify data imbalance, small targets, annotation burden, background complexity, uncertainty, and limited generalization as persistent issues, while adverse-weather object-detection research has developed explicit strategies such as image restoration, synthetic weather augmentation, unsupervised domain adaptation, teacher–student learning, and feature alignment. The review argues that the central unresolved problem is not simply increasing mAP under one condition, but maintaining dependable detection when the environmental distribution changes. A five-dimensional taxonomy and a deployment-oriented conceptual framework are proposed around environmental condition, adaptation strategy, detector architecture, deployment platform, and validation regime. The resulting research agenda emphasizes multi-weather benchmark datasets, cross-weather testing, domain generalization, uncertainty-aware detection, lightweight edge inference, multimodal sensing, and continual adaptation.
Keywords: animal detection; wildlife monitoring; quadruped detection; deep learning; YOLO; adverse weather; domain adaptation; domain generalization; computer vision; edge AI

1 Introduction

Automated animal detection has moved from a niche computer-vision problem to an increasingly practical component of ecological monitoring, precision livestock farming, transport safety, and human–wildlife conflict mitigation. Camera traps, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), fixed surveillance cameras, mobile robots, and other remote sensors now generate large volumes of imagery that cannot be inspected manually at scale. Recent reviews report rapid adoption of CNNs, YOLO-family detectors, Faster R-CNN, and transformer-based models across wildlife and livestock applications [1–4].

The performance numbers reported in individual studies can be impressive, but they are not directly comparable because datasets, animal classes, image scales, environments, annotation protocols, and evaluation procedures differ. Xu et al. [1], for example, reviewed animal detection in aerial and satellite imagery and identified recurring problems involving class imbalance, small samples, small objects, annotation, background, counting, accuracy assessment, and uncertainty. Raza et al. [2] similarly synthesized a broad literature and highlighted cross-geographical generalization and deployment constraints. These findings suggest that model architecture alone does not define the reliability of an animal detector.

Environmental variability makes the problem harder. A wildlife detector may encounter rain droplets, fog, haze, snow, dust, shadows, vegetation changes, low-light noise, motion blur, and camera-specific artifacts. In a clear-to-adverse-weather transfer setting, these effects constitute a distribution shift: the visual statistics of the target domain differ from those represented during training. Research in adverse-weather object detection has therefore increasingly treated weather as a domain adaptation problem rather than merely an image-quality problem [5–8].

The distinctive focus of this review is the intersection of these two streams. Existing reviews separately address animal detection and adverse-weather object detection, but the practical research question is narrower and more demanding: how can a multi-class quadruped detector maintain useful performance when both the animal domain and the environmental domain change? This review therefore emphasizes cross-weather generalization, domain adaptation, data diversity, deployment constraints, and evaluation protocols rather than simply ranking detector architectures.

2 Review Objectives and Research Questions

The review is designed to answer the following questions: (1) How has deep learning been applied to multi-class quadruped animal detection? (2) Which detection architectures and learning strategies dominate current animal-detection research? (3) How do weather and environmental changes affect animal-detection performance? (4) Which techniques improve robustness under adverse weather? (5) How effective are data augmentation, image restoration, domain adaptation, and domain generalization? (6) Which datasets and validation protocols are available for evaluating cross-weather animal detection? (7) What gaps prevent reliable field deployment? (8) What architecture and research agenda can guide weather-adaptive multi-class quadruped detection?

3 Literature Search and Filtering Method

A broad scholarly search was conducted using combinations of the terms animal detection, wildlife detection, livestock detection, quadruped detection, deep learning, YOLO, object detection, weather, adverse weather, rain, fog, snow, low light, domain adaptation, domain generalization, and robustness. Searches were cross-checked across academic discovery sources. The search was intentionally broader than the final inclusion scope because weather-adaptive animal detection is a relatively narrow intersection of two mature research streams.

When the candidate literature exceeds 50 papers, the review applies a relevance filter rather than attempting to cite every retrieved record. Priority is given to: (i) peer-reviewed studies; (ii) systematic or comprehensive reviews; (iii) studies with explicit animal-detection experiments; (iv) studies addressing environmental or domain variation; (v) studies reporting reproducible evaluation metrics; and (vi) recent work that changes the methodological landscape. General adverse-weather object-detection papers are retained when their methods directly inform weather adaptation, but they are not treated as animal-specific evidence.

4 State of Research in Animal Detection

The animal-detection literature shows a clear progression from conventional image processing and handcrafted features toward CNN-based detectors, one-stage real-time detectors, lightweight architectures, and transformer-enhanced systems. YOLO has become particularly common because animal monitoring frequently requires near-real-time inference. The 2024 review by Xu et al. [1] found YOLO, Faster R-CNN, U-Net, and ResNet among the most frequently used structures in animal detection from aerial and satellite imagery. The more recent review by Raza et al. [2], which screened 142 studies, likewise describes a progression toward modern YOLO and transformer-enhanced models while emphasizing deployment and cross-domain generalization.

At the application level, studies demonstrate that model specialization can produce strong performance. Li et al. [9] developed TMS-YOLO for wildlife detection and reported higher mAP than the YOLOv7 baseline on two datasets. Ma et al. [10] proposed WL-YOLO for complex forest scenes and reported high detection performance with a substantial reduction in parameters and detection time. Roy et al. [11] proposed WilDect-YOLO for endangered wildlife and reported 96.89% mAP and 59.20 FPS on its custom dataset. These results show the value of architectural adaptation, but they also illustrate a recurring limitation: performance is usually tied to a particular dataset and environmental setting.

The problem becomes visible when studies move closer to deployment. Tan et al. [12] compared several mainstream detectors on camera-trap data and found that joint day–night training could perform well, while Ibraheam et al. [13] explicitly optimized a YOLOv2-derived detector for embedded deployment. Adam et al. [14] reviewed UAV livestock detection and highlighted the trade-off among precision, recall, mAP, inference speed, and model size. Thus, a useful weather-adaptive detector must optimize more than one metric: it must balance accuracy, latency, computational resources, and generalization.

5 Weather as a Domain-Shift Problem

Weather affects detection at several levels. Rain can create streaks, reflections, blur, and contrast loss; fog and haze reduce visibility and suppress high-frequency information; snow can alter brightness, texture, and background similarity; dust can introduce color and contrast changes; and low-light scenes increase noise and reduce discriminative detail. Seasonal change can produce a second-order effect through vegetation, ground cover, and background structure. Consequently, weather robustness is not equivalent to image enhancement. The detector must learn representations that preserve animal-specific evidence while becoming less sensitive to environmental nuisance factors.

Adverse-weather object-detection research has developed several approaches. DA-RAW separates a style gap from a weather gap and combines attention-based style handling with contrastive learning for robust adaptation [5]. TTSDA-YOLO treats adverse-weather detection as a domain-adaptation problem and uses a two-stage training framework [6]. SDA-DETR progressively aligns domains at image, feature, and semantic levels using a DETR architecture [7]. More recent work has explored teacher–student adversarial learning with pseudo-labels for foggy, rainy, and low-light conditions [8]. These methods are mostly evaluated on traffic scenes, so their direct transfer to animals remains an open empirical question.

6 Animal-Specific Evidence for Environmental Robustness

Animal-specific research already contains important pieces of the weather-adaptive problem. Kaur et al. [15] developed PAW for wildlife prediction under adverse outdoor conditions, combining dehazing and object detection in a robotic setting intended for railway-track monitoring. This is particularly relevant because it moves beyond a static benchmark toward real-time operation in mist, haze, smog, and fog. WildlifeRailGuard research likewise reports the practical difficulty of partial visibility and reduced accuracy in heavy rain or fog, with infrared sensing and enhancement used as mitigation strategies [16].

The livestock literature provides another perspective. Open-pasture monitoring is more environmentally variable than indoor barn monitoring. Recent work on SnowLivestockDet reports severe degradation in nighttime and heavy-snow subsets and explicitly frames the problem as a combination of low-quality observations and scarcity of extreme-weather training samples [17]. This suggests a useful distinction for future research: some weather failures are caused by degraded inputs, while others arise because the model has rarely seen the relevant domain during training.

Wildlife monitoring also demonstrates that domain shift is broader than weather. Active-learning research has shown that a small number of carefully selected labels can substantially reduce the effort needed to transfer an animal detector to a new UAV dataset [18]. Recent wildlife representation learning has begun to address geographical domain shift [19], while in-situ adaptation for camera traps has explored on-device adjustment to changing lighting, weather, and seasonal conditions [20]. These studies imply that a practical weather-adaptive system should treat weather as one component of a larger environmental-drift problem.

7 Detector Architectures: What the Literature Supports

The evidence does not support a universal claim that one detector family is best for weather-adaptive animal detection. YOLO-family models are attractive because they offer a favorable speed–accuracy trade-off and are already widely used in wildlife and livestock applications [1,2,9–14]. DETR introduced a fundamentally different set-prediction formulation and global attention mechanism [21], while later transformer-based systems have been investigated for adverse-weather adaptation [7]. The more defensible conclusion is that architecture selection should be conditioned on the deployment setting and adaptation mechanism.

8 Weather-Adaptation Strategies

No single adaptation strategy solves all forms of domain shift. Data augmentation is inexpensive and can improve exposure to rare conditions, but synthetic weather may not reproduce real atmospheric effects. Image restoration can improve visibility, yet it may remove or distort cues useful for detection. Domain adaptation explicitly addresses distribution differences but often assumes access to unlabeled target-domain images. Teacher–student approaches can exploit such images but are sensitive to pseudo-label quality. Multimodal sensing can provide complementary information, especially at night or in fog, but increases cost and deployment complexity.

9 Critical Research Gaps

The strongest gap is methodological rather than architectural. The field needs a common evaluation protocol that deliberately separates within-domain performance from cross-domain robustness. A model that obtains high mAP on a clear-weather test set but degrades severely on unseen fog should not be described as robust simply because its pooled benchmark score is high. Robustness should be reported as a function of environmental shift.

Priority gaps include standardized multi-weather benchmarks, unseen-weather testing, separation of weather from habitat/geographical shift, multi-class evaluation including rare species, external field validation, joint accuracy–latency–memory reporting, uncertainty calibration, and safe continual adaptation.

10 Proposed Five-Dimensional Taxonomy

This review proposes a taxonomy with five linked dimensions. First, the environmental dimension identifies clear, rain, fog/haze, snow, dust, night/low-light, and mixed conditions. Second, the adaptation dimension covers augmentation, restoration, domain adaptation, domain generalization, test-time/continual adaptation, and multimodal fusion. Third, the architecture dimension covers CNN-based, YOLO-based, two-stage, transformer-based, and hybrid detectors. Fourth, the deployment dimension distinguishes cloud/server, edge GPU, edge CPU, embedded, UAV, robot, and camera-trap settings. Fifth, the validation dimension distinguishes same-weather testing, cross-weather testing, cross-location testing, cross-camera testing, external validation, and field trials.

11 Proposed Weather-Adaptive Detection Framework

The literature supports a deployment-oriented pipeline rather than a detector-only architecture. The proposed framework begins with environmental sensing and image-quality assessment, followed by optional weather-aware preprocessing. A detector then extracts multi-scale features and predicts animal classes and locations. A domain-shift monitor estimates whether the current scene differs materially from the training distribution. When confidence is adequate, the system returns detections; when uncertainty is high, it should abstain, request a better frame, or route the case for human review. Longer-term adaptation can occur through offline retraining, controlled test-time adaptation, or continual learning.

12 Evaluation Protocol Recommended for Future Studies

Future papers should report more than a single mAP value. At minimum, the evaluation should contain a favorable-weather baseline and separate results for each adverse condition. The most informative design is a matrix in which the training domain and testing domain are explicitly identified: clear→clear, clear→rain, clear→fog, clear→night, multi-weather→unseen-weather, and multi-weather→new-location. Performance degradation should be reported rather than hidden by pooled averages.

Core metrics should include precision, recall, F1, mAP@0.5, mAP@0.5:0.95, IoU, FPS or latency, model parameters, and memory footprint. For safety-oriented systems, confidence calibration and false-negative analysis are particularly important. Per-class performance should also be reported because a strong overall score can hide systematic failure on rare species.

13 Future Research Agenda

A high-value research program should combine: (i) a multi-weather, multi-species benchmark; (ii) cross-weather domain generalization; (iii) weather-severity modeling; (iv) lightweight adaptation for edge devices; (v) uncertainty-aware detection and abstention; (vi) RGB–thermal or RGB–IR fusion; (vii) continual environmental adaptation; and (viii) geographically separated field validation.

14 Discussion

The literature supports a shift in how the problem should be framed. Earlier animal-detection studies understandably focused on increasing detection accuracy through better feature extraction, attention modules, feature pyramids, and lightweight architectures. That work remains valuable, but the current research frontier is increasingly about generalization. Recent animal reviews explicitly identify cross-geographical generalization, background variation, data imbalance, and deployment limitations [1,2]. Recent domain-adaptation research in adverse weather provides a mature methodological toolkit [5–8], but much of that evidence comes from road scenes rather than wildlife or livestock.

This creates an opportunity for a focused research program rather than another incremental YOLO modification. A publishable next step would be to construct or curate a multi-weather quadruped dataset, define a strict cross-weather benchmark, compare augmentation against domain adaptation and domain generalization, and evaluate both accuracy and deployment cost. The resulting contribution would be more defensible than claiming a new detector merely because it improves mAP on one custom dataset.

Weather should not be isolated from other environmental shifts. In real deployments, rain may coincide with a new background, a different camera, lower light, seasonal vegetation, or a geographic change. A model can therefore appear weather-robust while actually exploiting background correlations. Controlled factorization of weather, location, camera, season, and habitat is essential for understanding what the model has learned.

15 Limitations of This Review

The review is based on broad scholarly discovery and accessible academic records rather than a direct export from a licensed Scopus database. The literature is heterogeneous, and many reported performance values are not directly comparable. The review therefore emphasizes methodological patterns and research gaps rather than constructing a formal meta-analysis of model performance.

16 Conclusion

Weather-adaptive multi-class quadruped detection is best understood as a robustness and domain-shift problem rather than simply an object-detection architecture problem. Deep-learning models, especially YOLO-family detectors, have demonstrated strong results in wildlife and livestock monitoring, while transformer-based and domain-adaptive methods are expanding the available design space. Nevertheless, the literature remains fragmented with respect to standardized cross-weather benchmarks, unseen-weather testing, external validation, uncertainty, and edge deployment.

Future work should connect five elements: environmental diversity, adaptation strategy, detector architecture, deployment platform, and validation regime. The most valuable research contribution is likely to come from evaluating whether a detector can retain reliable recall and calibrated confidence when the weather, camera, location, and season change. A system that can detect animals accurately in favorable images is useful; a system that knows when weather has made its prediction unreliable is much closer to dependable field intelligence.
