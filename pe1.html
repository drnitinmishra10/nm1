Beyond Effective Prompts: Robustness, Security, Transferability and Reliability in Prompt Engineering

A reliability-centred research agenda and empirical framework for Large Language Models
Dr. Nitin Mishra
Professor, Department of Computer Science and Engineering
Parul Institute of Engineering and Technology (PIET), Parul University, Vadodara, Gujarat, India
Manuscript status. This is a literature-grounded review/framework manuscript. Findings reported as established are attributed to prior studies. The RSTR-Prompt framework, metrics, hypotheses, benchmark design, and experimental protocol are proposed contributions and require empirical validation before being presented as demonstrated results.

Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have changed the way artificial intelligence systems are designed, accessed and deployed. A significant part of their practical adaptation now takes place through natural-language prompts rather than changes to model parameters. Prompt engineering has consequently moved from an informal practice of instruction writing to a substantial research field covering few-shot learning, reasoning elicitation, retrieval augmentation, self-refinement, tool use and automatic prompt optimisation. Yet high performance on a selected benchmark should not be confused with reliability. Existing studies demonstrate sensitivity to wording and demonstration order, instability across models, positional effects in long contexts, multilingual performance differences, evaluation artefacts and vulnerabilities to prompt injection. These observations suggest that prompt quality is inherently multi-dimensional. A prompt may be accurate but brittle, transferable but expensive, effective but insecure, or reliable on one language while degrading in another. This paper develops a critical research synthesis around five dimensions—robustness, security, transferability, reliability and efficiency—and proposes RSTR-Prompt, a reliability-centred framework for prompt design, stress testing and lifecycle management. The framework treats prompt selection as a multi-objective problem rather than as the maximisation of a single task score. It introduces a controlled prompt-perturbation protocol, cross-model and cross-language validation, context-position testing, adversarial injection testing, reproducibility requirements and cost-aware evaluation. The paper also proposes research questions, hypotheses, quantitative metrics and an empirical protocol suitable for implementation across heterogeneous LLMs and task families. A dedicated Indian multilingual research track is proposed for English, Hindi, Gujarati and code-mixed prompts, reflecting practical conditions in Indian AI deployments. The principal argument is that the next stage of prompt engineering should move beyond finding prompts that work and instead establish engineering methods for prompts that continue to work under reasonable changes in models, inputs, languages, context, evaluation and threat conditions.

Keywords: Large Language Models; Prompt Engineering; Prompt Robustness; Prompt Sensitivity; Prompt Injection; Transferability; Reliability; Multilingual LLMs; Automatic Prompt Optimization; LLM Security; Context Engineering; Responsible AI

1. Introduction

The rapid development of Large Language Models has created a distinctive mode of software interaction in which natural language itself becomes an important control interface. Earlier NLP pipelines generally required explicit feature engineering, task-specific training or fine-tuning. The GPT-3 study demonstrated that scaling a language model could enable strong few-shot task adaptation through textual instructions and examples without gradient updates for the downstream task [1]. This was more than a performance milestone. It established the practical possibility of treating an LLM as a broadly programmable system through natural-language input.

The subsequent literature made the control problem more explicit. Chain-of-Thought prompting showed that intermediate reasoning examples can elicit stronger performance on several complex reasoning benchmarks [3]. Least-to-Most prompting decomposed difficult problems into simpler subproblems [5], while Self-Consistency sampled multiple reasoning paths to improve answer selection [6]. ReAct combined reasoning with interaction with external information sources [9], and later approaches such as Self-Refine and Reflexion introduced iterative feedback and memory [10], [11]. Tree of Thoughts generalised the idea of reasoning beyond a single chain by exploring multiple candidate paths [12].

The natural reaction to this progress is to search for better prompts. However, the literature increasingly shows that a prompt should not be treated as a universally effective string. Lu et al. demonstrated that merely changing the order of few-shot demonstrations could move performance from near state-of-the-art to close to random-guess behaviour on some tasks, and they found that good permutations were not necessarily transferable across models [7]. Recent work has studied prompt sensitivity directly, including ProSA, PromptSET and a large-scale 2025 analysis of robustness to non-semantic formatting changes [28]–[31].

The issue is also broader than prompt wording. LLMs can use long contexts imperfectly; Liu et al. found that relevant information placed in the middle of long inputs can be under-utilised [22]. Security studies show that untrusted retrieved content can act as an injection channel [23], and dedicated prompt-injection benchmarks have revealed significant instruction-following vulnerabilities [24]. Multilingual research further shows that zero-shot cross-lingual transfer and translated evaluation are not straightforward substitutes for native-language evaluation [26], [27].

These observations motivate the title of this paper. The practical question is no longer simply whether a prompt is effective. The more consequential question is whether it is robust, secure, transferable and reliable under realistic operating conditions. This distinction is particularly important in higher education, healthcare, public services, enterprise knowledge systems and other applications where an incorrect or manipulated answer may be more costly than a small decrease in benchmark accuracy.

Central proposition. Prompt quality should be treated as a multidimensional engineering property. High benchmark accuracy is necessary in many applications, but it is not sufficient evidence of prompt reliability.

The contribution of this paper is therefore not another catalogue of prompting techniques. Recent surveys already provide extensive catalogues. The Prompt Report, for example, identifies 58 LLM prompting techniques and a common vocabulary [20]. Liu et al.'s 2026 taxonomy organises prompt engineering into profile and instruction, knowledge, reasoning and planning, and reliability [32]. Ramnath et al.'s 2025 EMNLP survey provides a formal framework for automatic prompt optimisation [29]. The present paper builds on this foundation and asks what comes after technique accumulation: how prompts should be stress-tested, compared across models and languages, and managed as software-like components over time.

The remainder of the paper first defines the research problem and review approach, then synthesises the literature around the major challenge dimensions. A reliability-centred RSTR-Prompt framework is subsequently proposed, together with metrics, hypotheses, benchmark design and statistical methods. The paper closes with threats to validity, an Indian multilingual research agenda, and a set of practical recommendations for empirical researchers.

2. Research Problem and Objectives

2.1 From single-objective prompt optimisation to reliability

A common abstraction of prompt optimisation is to choose the prompt that maximises task performance on a validation set.

\[p^{*}=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}} A(p)\]

Here, \(\mathcal{P}\) is the candidate prompt space and \(A(p)\) is an application-specific performance metric. This formulation is useful for comparing prompting methods, and automatic prompt optimisation systems such as APE, OPRO, EvoPrompt and DSPy demonstrate that search over prompt space can be automated [14]–[18].

Nevertheless, a deployment prompt is exposed to factors not represented in the single score: paraphrases, example permutations, context-position changes, new models, new languages, malicious inputs, decoding stochasticity and inference costs. A more realistic objective is therefore:

\[p^{*}=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}} F\!\left(A(p),R(p),T(p),Q(p),S(p),C(p)\right)\]

where \(R\) denotes robustness, \(T\) transferability, \(Q\) reliability, \(S\) security resilience, and \(C\) cost. The function \(F\) can be a weighted utility or a Pareto-front analysis. The framework proposed later intentionally does not impose universal weights because the relative importance of accuracy, security, latency and cost depends on application risk.

2.2 Objectives

The paper has four objectives. First, to consolidate the evidence concerning prompt sensitivity, transferability, long-context effects, multilinguality, security and evaluation. Second, to identify the research gaps that remain after the rapid growth of prompting surveys and automated optimisation. Third, to propose a unified empirical framework for measuring prompt reliability. Fourth, to define a reproducible experimental protocol that can be implemented without relying on proprietary internal model information.

4. Evolution of Prompt Engineering

The literature can be read as a progression from prompting as task specification toward prompting as system optimisation.

This progression provides the intellectual basis for the present work. The field's maturity is visible not only in the number of prompting techniques but also in the emergence of dedicated survey and benchmark literature. The Prompt Report catalogues techniques [20]; PromptBench provides reusable evaluation infrastructure [21]; PromptSET treats prompt sensitivity as a dedicated prediction problem [28]; the 2025 APO survey consolidates automatic optimisation research [29]; and the 2026 taxonomy explicitly includes reliability as a core dimension [32].

5. Major Research Challenges

5.1 Prompt sensitivity and brittleness

Prompt sensitivity refers to performance changes produced by small, ideally semantics-preserving, modifications of a prompt. Lu et al. demonstrated substantial sensitivity to few-shot example ordering [7]. ProSA introduced a dedicated sensitivity metric and found that sensitivity varies across datasets and models [28]. PromptSET formalised prompt-sensitivity prediction and reported that existing approaches still struggle to predict which prompt variants will perform well [28]. A 2025 large-scale study further showed that punctuation and formatting changes can matter across numerous models and tasks [30].

Research opportunity: The core research gap is to optimise prompts not only for their mean score but also for low variance across controlled perturbations.
\[PSI=1-\frac{\sigma\left(M(p_1),\ldots,M(p_n)\right)}{\mu\left(M(p_1),\ldots,M(p_n)\right)+\epsilon}\]

5.2 Demonstration selection and ordering

Few-shot prompts introduce a combinatorial design space. If a prompt contains k demonstrations, the possible orderings alone scale as k!. Lu et al. show that this apparently superficial choice can have large effects [7]. The implication is that example order should be regarded as an experimental variable rather than an incidental formatting decision.

Research opportunity: Future work should jointly optimise demonstration selection, ordering and robustness across models.
\[DO(p)=\operatorname{Var}_{\pi\in\Pi_k}\left[M\!\left(p;\pi(D)\right)\right]\]

5.3 Cross-model transferability

A prompt optimised for one model can exploit model-specific instruction preferences. OPRO, EvoPrompt and DSPy demonstrate substantial gains on their evaluated targets, but optimisation success on a target model does not itself establish portability [16]–[18]. Prompt-order studies already show that good prompt configurations can fail to transfer across models [7].

Research opportunity: The practical requirement is to evaluate prompts on unseen or held-out model families rather than treating the optimisation model as representative of all LLMs.
\[CTS(p)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_k(p)\]

5.4 Cross-task transferability

Prompt components such as role descriptions, reasoning scaffolds, examples and output schemas have task-specific effects. A reasoning-intensive prompt may be useful for mathematics and unnecessary for structured extraction. This motivates ablation rather than intuition-driven claims about universally good prompt components.

Research opportunity: The research objective is to identify prompt components whose utility persists across task families.
\[\Delta_i=M(P)-M(P\setminus i)\]

5.5 Long-context and positional robustness

Long context is valuable, but the ability to use long context is not uniform. Liu et al. showed that relevant evidence in the middle of long inputs can be under-used compared with information near the beginning or end [22]. Prompt-injection research also reports that models may disproportionately attend to later instructions under attack [24].

Research opportunity: Prompt evaluation should vary both context length and evidence position.
\[PRS=1-\frac{\sigma(M_{position})}{\mu(M_{position})+\epsilon}\]

5.6 Reliability, hallucination and self-verification

Reasoning scaffolds can improve performance, while retrieval and tool interaction can improve grounding [9]. Self-Refine demonstrates that iterative feedback can improve outputs [10]. Yet self-verification is not the same as independent verification: a model may repeat a shared reasoning or factual error during generation and checking.

Research opportunity: Research should compare self-checking against independent evidence, external retrieval, deterministic validators or human evaluation.
\[Rel=\lambda_1Fact+\lambda_2Cons+\lambda_3Ver+\lambda_4Stab\]

5.7 Security and prompt injection

Prompt injection exploits the fact that system instructions, user content and retrieved text may be represented in a common natural-language context. Greshake et al. demonstrated indirect injection through retrieved content [23]. Li et al. established a benchmark for instruction-following robustness under injection and found significant vulnerabilities [24]. Security surveys now treat prompt injection and jailbreaking as central LLM risks [33].

Research opportunity: Prompt-level defences should be evaluated as one layer in a larger security architecture. They should not be treated as a complete security control.
\[ASR=\frac{N_{successful\ attacks}}{N_{total\ attacks}}\]

5.8 Multilingual and code-mixed prompting

Cross-lingual instruction following can differ by language and evaluation setup [26], and translation-based multilingual evaluation can hide language-specific behaviour [27]. For India, the issue is extended by code-mixed communication such as Hinglish and regional-language interaction.

Research opportunity: The open problem is prompt equivalence: two prompts may be semantically similar while differing pragmatically and operationally across languages.
\[\mathrm{LTR}=\frac{M_{target\ language}(p)}{M_{English}(p)+\epsilon}\]

5.9 Bias induced or amplified by instruction

Instruction-tuned models can display cognitive biases in decision and reasoning tasks [25]. Prompt wording may therefore act as a moderator of model-level bias even when the underlying task is unchanged.

Research opportunity: Prompt audits should include counterfactual or demographic perturbations where appropriate to the application.
\[B_p=\max_{g_i,g_j}\left|M(p,g_i)-M(p,g_j)\right|\]

5.10 Evaluation validity

Prompt sensitivity research raises a methodological point: the evaluation method itself can change measured variance. A 2025 study argues that some apparent prompt sensitivity can be amplified by rigid exact-match or likelihood-based evaluation, while semantic judging can reduce some variance [31]. This does not eliminate sensitivity, but it warns against interpreting every score fluctuation as a property of the model alone.

Research opportunity: Prompt studies need task-appropriate metrics and should report evaluator choice as part of the experimental condition.
\[E_{observed}=f(M,p,D,Evaluator)\]

5.11 Reproducibility and version drift

Prompt results depend on model version, decoding parameters, data order, context and sometimes provider-side changes. A prompt is therefore closer to a versioned software component than a permanent text string.

Research opportunity: A prompt record should include exact model version, prompt version, decoding configuration, datasets, examples, evaluation code and date of validation.
\[Y=f(p,model,version,D,decode,context,evaluator)\]

5.12 Optimisation overfitting

Automatic optimisers can evaluate very large numbers of candidate prompts. Selecting the maximum development-set score creates a search-overfitting risk: the more prompts that are tested, the greater the opportunity for a candidate to benefit from idiosyncrasies of the optimisation data.

Research opportunity: Prompt optimisation should use held-out test data, perturbation stress tests and preferably cross-model validation.
\[p^*=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}M_{dev}(p)\;\not\Rightarrow\;p^*=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}M_{deployment}(p)\]

5.13 Cost and latency

Reasoning ensembles, self-refinement and prompt optimisation can improve performance but require additional tokens and calls. A production prompt therefore has an economic profile as well as a task score.

Research opportunity: Evaluation should report performance per token, per call, per unit cost and per unit latency where possible.
\[CE=\frac{A}{\alpha Tokens+\beta Calls+\gamma Latency}\]

6. Evidence Synthesis: What the Literature Establishes

The evidence base does not support a single story that all prompt changes cause severe instability. Instead, it supports a conditional view. Some studies find substantial prompt sensitivity; others suggest that measured sensitivity can depend on task, model size, evaluation method and the type of perturbation. This nuance is important for a credible journal paper.

6.1 Robustness is measurable but heterogeneous

ProSA evaluates prompt sensitivity across multiple tasks and models and reports variation in sensitivity, with larger models showing greater robustness in its settings [28]. PromptSET similarly treats sensitivity prediction as a non-trivial research task [28]. Seleznyov et al. extend robustness analysis to 8 models and 52 Natural Instructions tasks, with perturbations involving prompt phrasing and formatting [30]. These studies make the case for dedicated stress testing, but they do not justify a universal numerical robustness threshold.

6.2 Evaluation can itself produce apparent instability

Hua et al. revisit prompt sensitivity and argue that part of the observed variance can arise from evaluation artefacts, especially when exact-match or likelihood-based metrics fail to recognise semantically equivalent answers [31]. This finding is highly relevant to our framework: a robust prompt benchmark must separate model instability from evaluator instability. In practice, this suggests combining objective metrics with semantic evaluation and a human-audited subset.

6.3 Security creates a competing optimisation objective

Prompt injection cannot be reduced to a simple instruction-writing error. The indirect injection literature shows that instructions can enter systems through data sources [23]. The EMNLP 2024 benchmark shows that stronger instruction-following can coexist with vulnerability to embedded malicious instructions [24]. The result is a genuine systems trade-off: a model must follow trusted instructions faithfully while resisting instructions that originate in untrusted content.

6.4 The field has moved toward automatic prompt optimisation

APE, OPRO, Promptbreeder, EvoPrompt, PromptAgent and DSPy collectively demonstrate that prompt discovery is becoming algorithmic [14]–[19]. Ramnath et al.'s 2025 survey formalises APO as a growing research area and identifies a wide range of design choices [29]. The consequence is that prompt engineering now inherits familiar optimisation concerns: objective design, search efficiency, generalisation, overfitting and cost.

6.5 Reliability is entering the mainstream taxonomy

The 2026 Frontiers of Computer Science review explicitly places reliability alongside profile and instruction, knowledge, and reasoning and planning [32]. This is closely aligned with the present argument. The contribution here is to connect reliability to empirical stress testing and cross-condition transfer, rather than treating reliability only as a prompt-writing category.

7. Proposed RSTR-Prompt Framework

This paper proposes RSTR-Prompt: Robust, Secure, Transferable and Reliable Prompt Engineering. The framework is designed as a lifecycle rather than as a single prompt template.

7.1 Stage A: Task and risk definition

The first step is to define what the system is expected to do and what constitutes an unacceptable failure. A creative writing assistant may tolerate stylistic variation. A medical information assistant should not silently invent evidence. A system connected to external tools should treat instruction injection as a security-critical failure. Risk classification therefore determines which dimensions receive greater weight in optimisation and acceptance testing.

7.2 Stage B: Baseline prompt construction

\[P_{base}=I+C+K+O+V\]

where \(I\) is instruction, \(C\) is context, \(K\) is constraints, \(O\) is output specification and \(V\) is an optional verification stage. The baseline should be intentionally simple so that later gains and failure modes can be attributed to specific interventions.

7.3 Stage C: Candidate generation

Candidate prompts can be authored by humans, generated by an LLM, mutated by an evolutionary optimiser, or compiled from a programmatic specification. A diverse candidate pool is preferable to a single optimisation family because search methods have different inductive biases.

7.4 Stage D: Multi-objective optimisation

\[J(p)=w_AA(p)+w_RR(p)+w_TT(p)+w_QQ(p)+w_SS(p)-w_CC(p)\]

The weights are application-specific. Alternatively, researchers can avoid collapsing the objectives and report a Pareto frontier. This is particularly useful when one prompt has slightly higher accuracy but another has much lower cost and injection success.

7.5 Stage E: Perturbation stress testing

Candidate prompts should be subjected to lexical, syntactic, structural, demonstration-order, context-position, verbosity and language perturbations. The test suite should distinguish semantics-preserving perturbations from genuine task changes. The former are used to measure robustness; the latter are used to test distribution shift.

7.6 Stage F: Cross-model, multilingual and security validation

The same prompt artefact should be evaluated on more than one model family and, where relevant, more than one language. Security tests should include both direct and indirect injection. For agentic systems, the test should follow the whole trajectory: prompt → tool call → retrieved content → subsequent reasoning → action.

7.7 Stage G: Deployment and lifecycle monitoring

A validated prompt should be versioned and monitored. When the underlying model changes, the prompt should undergo regression testing rather than being assumed to retain its previous properties.

8. Formal Evaluation Model

8.1 Robustness

Let \(\mathcal{P}_{sem}(p)\) be a set of prompts judged semantically equivalent to base prompt \(p\). A robustness evaluation can consider both the mean score and the dispersion of performance across this set.

\[\bar{A}_{sem}(p)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(p_i),\quad p_i\in\mathcal{P}_{sem}(p)\]
\[Var_{sem}(p)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(A(p_i)-\bar{A}_{sem}(p)\right)^2\]

8.2 Cross-model transfer

\[T_{model}(p)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_k(p),\qquad V_{model}(p)=\operatorname{Var}_{k}(A_k(p))\]

A prompt intended for portability should ideally show both acceptable average performance and limited model-to-model variance. A held-out model family can be reserved specifically to test transfer.

8.3 Language transfer

\[T_{lang}(p,l)=\frac{A(p,l)}{A(p,English)+\epsilon}\]

For Indian multilingual research, the language set can include English, Hindi, Gujarati and a code-mixed form such as Hinglish. The study should distinguish direct translation from native or naturally code-mixed prompt authoring.

8.4 Security

\[ASR=\frac{N_{successful\ attacks}}{N_{attacks}}\]
\[U_{secure}=A-\lambda\,ASR-\gamma\,Latency\]

Security evaluation should not only count whether an attack succeeded. The downstream consequence should also be scored where possible, for example whether an injection merely changes formatting or actually causes an unauthorised tool call.

8.5 Cost and efficiency

\[CE=\frac{A}{c_tT+c_cN_{calls}+c_lL}\]

Here \(T\) is token usage, \(N_{calls}\) is the number of model calls, \(L\) is latency, and the coefficients represent application-specific cost scaling. The framework is deliberately dimensionless at the conceptual stage; operational studies should use actual token price and latency observations.

A heterogeneous model set is recommended. 10.5 Experimental controls

  • Freeze model identifiers and record provider/model versions.
  • Use the same dataset split for all prompt families.
  • Report temperature, top-p, max output tokens and other decoding parameters.
  • Use held-out evaluation data and prevent prompt search from accessing the final test set.
  • Run repeated stochastic trials where the inference configuration is non-deterministic.
  • Archive prompts, demonstrations, evaluation scripts and raw outputs.

10.6 Statistical analysis

For stochastic models, the study should report mean and standard deviation across repeated runs. Pairwise comparisons can use paired tests where the same items are evaluated under two conditions. Wilcoxon signed-rank testing is suitable when distributional assumptions are doubtful; paired t-tests may be used where approximate normality is justified. For more complex designs spanning multiple models, tasks and prompt families, repeated-measures or mixed-effects models are preferable. Effect sizes and confidence intervals should accompany p-values.

Multiple-comparison correction should be applied when a large number of prompt families, models and perturbations are tested. A pre-registered analysis plan would further strengthen the paper by reducing researcher degrees of freedom.

11. Ablation and Benchmark Design

11.1 Prompt-component ablation

Let the full prompt contain instruction, examples, reasoning scaffold, verification instruction, constraints and output schema. Remove one component at a time and measure the change in each objective.

\[\Delta_i=M(P)-M(P\setminus i)\]

The important point is that ablations should be evaluated not only on accuracy. Removing a component could reduce accuracy slightly while improving robustness or security. A multi-objective analysis can therefore reveal which components create beneficial or harmful trade-offs.

11.2 The Robust Prompt Challenge benchmark

As a concrete follow-on contribution, the paper can release a benchmark provisionally named the Robust Prompt Challenge (RPC). Each benchmark item would contain a base task, a canonical prompt, a family of semantics-preserving variants, multiple context positions, multilingual variants where applicable, and an adversarial injection version for systems that process untrusted content.

13. Proposed Research Contributions

  1. A consolidated challenge taxonomy that places robustness, security, transferability, reliability and efficiency alongside established prompt-engineering techniques.
  2. RSTR-Prompt, a lifecycle framework for prompt design, optimisation, stress testing and monitoring.
  3. A controlled perturbation methodology for measuring sensitivity to lexical, syntactic, structural, positional and multilingual changes.
  4. A cross-model transfer protocol that separates in-domain optimisation from out-of-model generalisation.
  5. A security evaluation layer that treats direct and indirect prompt injection as deployment conditions rather than isolated red-team anecdotes.
  6. A multi-objective mathematical formulation for prompt selection that explicitly includes performance, robustness, transferability, reliability, security and cost.
  7. A reproducibility protocol for versioning prompts and recording model, decoding, data and evaluation configurations.
  8. An Indian multilingual benchmark agenda covering English, Hindi, Gujarati and code-mixed prompts as a practical test bed for prompt transfer and robustness.

14. Indian Multilingual Research Track

A distinctive opportunity for the proposed study is to test prompt robustness in Indian linguistic settings. India presents a useful research environment because many real interactions are multilingual and code-mixed. The prompt engineering problem is therefore not limited to translating an English instruction into another language; it includes natural-language variation, code mixing, domain terminology and culturally familiar forms of instruction.

This research track could support LLM applications in Indian higher education, public-service information, local-language customer support, educational tutoring, multilingual knowledge retrieval and institution-level AI assistants. Such applications make robustness particularly relevant because users may reformulate requests naturally rather than reproduce benchmark-style English prompts.

15. Discussion

The combined literature suggests that prompt engineering has reached a point at which descriptive technique catalogues are no longer sufficient as the sole research contribution. The prompt is increasingly embedded within a larger system: retrieved documents, tool descriptions, conversation history, memory, external evaluators and dynamic optimisation routines all influence the model's effective context. A prompt that works in isolation may behave differently once it enters this ecosystem.

The first implication is that robustness should be treated as a first-class metric. Lu et al.'s prompt-order findings [7], ProSA [28], PromptSET [28], and the 2025 punctuation study [30] collectively show that seemingly small changes can matter. At the same time, Hua et al. [31] remind us that some observed sensitivity may be produced or amplified by the evaluator. A mature benchmark should therefore use multiple evaluation modes and a small human-audited sample.

The second implication is that security and reliability are coupled. Better instruction following is generally valuable, but an LLM that obediently follows arbitrary instructions embedded in retrieved text can become more exploitable. Prompt injection research makes clear that natural-language separation between instruction and data is fragile [23], [24]. The correct design target is not blind obedience and not blanket refusal; it is context-sensitive instruction hierarchy supported by system-level controls.

The third implication is that transferability is likely to become more important as the model ecosystem diversifies. Organisations routinely compare proprietary and open-weight models, and the cost or governance profile of a deployment can change over time. A prompt that is tightly coupled to one model's quirks creates maintenance debt. This is why the proposed framework reserves part of validation for models that were not used during optimisation.

The fourth implication concerns cost. Self-consistency, self-refinement, search-based prompt optimisation and agentic reasoning can add inference calls. A research paper that reports only accuracy may therefore overstate practical value. At minimum, the number of model calls and total token usage should be reported. For commercial deployments, actual monetary cost and latency should also be included.

Finally, the Indian multilingual dimension can make the research more distinctive. A large part of the prompt literature is evaluated in English-centric benchmarks. Testing native Hindi and Gujarati prompts alongside code-mixed interaction provides a practical setting in which prompt transfer, robustness and cultural pragmatics can be examined together. This should be done experimentally rather than through anecdotal examples.

16. Threats to Validity and Limitations

ThreatImplication
Model coverageNo study can cover all current and future LLMs. Results must therefore be interpreted as conditional on the selected model families and versions.
Task coverageSix task families may not represent all enterprise or scientific applications. Conclusions should not be generalised beyond evaluated tasks without supporting evidence.
Prompt-space coveragePerturbation generators may themselves introduce artefacts. Human semantic validation is recommended for a subset of variants.
Evaluation validityNo single metric captures all dimensions of quality. Objective metrics, semantic evaluation and human auditing should therefore be combined.
Security realismSynthetic injection cases may differ from attacks observed in production systems. Agentic evaluation should use realistic tool and retrieval pathways where possible.
Temporal validityModel updates can change prompt behaviour. Results should always include evaluation dates and exact model identifiers.
Cost comparabilityToken prices and infrastructure differ by provider. The study should report raw token/call/latency values in addition to any normalised cost metric.
Language equivalenceSemantically equivalent prompts across languages can differ pragmatically. Native-speaker review is needed for strong multilingual claims.

18. Practical Research Protocol for the Next Study

For researchers intending to convert this manuscript into a fully empirical journal article, the following sequence is recommended.

  1. Freeze the literature corpus and run the final Scopus/Web of Science/ACL/IEEE search with the documented strings.
  2. Screen records using two independent reviewers for a sampled subset and record disagreements.
  3. Implement the perturbation engine and manually validate a representative sample of semantics-preserving variants.
  4. Select a heterogeneous model panel and freeze model versions and decoding parameters.
  5. Create a development set for optimisation and an untouched test set for final claims.
  6. Compare human, automatic, robustness-aware and cost-aware prompt optimisation.
  7. Run prompt, model, language, position and security stress tests.
  8. Apply statistical tests with effect sizes and confidence intervals.
  9. Release prompts, code, perturbation generators, evaluation scripts and a machine-readable results file where licensing permits.
  10. Write the results as observed evidence, not as anticipated outcomes.

19. Conclusion

Prompt engineering has progressed from a practical technique for interacting with language models into a broad research field encompassing reasoning, retrieval, self-refinement, agents and automatic optimisation. The next challenge is not to accumulate another list of prompting tricks, but to determine whether prompts remain dependable as the surrounding conditions change.

The literature already provides strong evidence for prompt-order sensitivity, prompt perturbation effects, long-context positional failures, multilingual transfer limitations, prompt-injection vulnerabilities and the rapid emergence of automatic prompt optimisation [7], [22]–[32]. At the same time, recent work warns that evaluation methodology can itself contribute to measured prompt sensitivity [31]. These results support a more careful and engineering-oriented conception of prompt quality.

The RSTR-Prompt framework proposed in this paper addresses that need by combining performance with robustness, security, transferability, reliability and efficiency. The framework does not claim that one metric can summarise all dimensions. Instead, it recommends a controlled test suite, multi-objective analysis, cross-model validation, multilingual evaluation and prompt lifecycle management.

For researchers, the practical message is simple. A prompt should not be accepted because it produces the highest score in one experiment. It should be examined for how its performance changes when the wording changes, examples are reordered, relevant evidence moves within the context, a different model is used, another language is introduced, untrusted text is injected or inference cost increases.

The future of prompt engineering is therefore likely to be less about finding a clever sentence and more about building dependable prompt systems. Such a shift would make prompt engineering closer to software engineering: versioned, tested, stress-tested, reproducible and continuously maintained. For Indian and multilingual AI applications, this transition is especially important because real users do not interact with LLMs in benchmark-perfect English. They switch languages, mix languages, shorten instructions, provide incomplete context and ask follow-up questions. A robust prompt engineering discipline must be designed for that reality.
