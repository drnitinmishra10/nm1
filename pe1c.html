Beyond Effective Prompts: A Security-Centric Taxonomy and Research Agenda for Prompt Engineering in Large Language Models
Dr. Nitin Mishra1, Dr. Rohit1
1Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Parul University, Vadodara, Gujarat, India
Corresponding author: Dr. Nitin Mishra, Professor, Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Parul University
Abstract— Prompt engineering has become an important way of improving the quality and consistency of large language model (LLM) responses, but prompts can also create security risks when attackers manipulate instructions, context, or external content. This paper presents a structured review of security threats in prompt-driven AI systems and organizes sixteen threats into a practical taxonomy covering prompt manipulation, information exposure, model and data integrity, retrieval security, application and agent security, multimodal attacks, and resource exhaustion. Recent academic studies, major conference publications, and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM and Generative AI Applications are examined to clarify the boundaries between closely related threats such as indirect prompt injection, context hijacking, sensitive information disclosure, and prompt-based data exfiltration. The paper further reviews defensive approaches, compares evidence across representative model studies, proposes measurable agent-security metrics, and discusses provenance-aware prompt construction. The results indicate that secure prompting cannot rely on wording alone; effective protection requires layered controls spanning prompts, retrieval, authorization, tool execution, monitoring, and governance. Future work is proposed around multilingual attacks, long-context systems, multimodal security, adaptive defenses, and reproducible evaluation.
Keywords: prompt engineering; prompt injection; indirect prompt injection; jailbreak; system prompt leakage; LLM security; RAG security; AI agents; multimodal security; trustworthy AI.

1 Introduction

Large language models have moved quickly from experimental systems to components of everyday software. They are used to summarize documents, answer questions, write and review code, search internal knowledge, support customer interactions, and increasingly operate as agents that can call tools and perform multi-step tasks. Prompt engineering sits at the centre of these applications because prompts define the task, provide context, constrain the response, and often describe what the model is expected to do.

That usefulness also creates a security problem. A prompt is not simply an instruction written by a trusted developer. In a modern application, the model may receive a mixture of system instructions, user text, retrieved documents, email content, tool output, and visual information. Much of that material is untrusted, yet it may appear to the model as ordinary natural language. When the model cannot reliably separate authority from content, an attacker can use language itself as an attack surface [1–5].

Research has demonstrated that the problem is practical. InjecAgent evaluated 30 tool-integrated LLM agents across 1,054 test cases involving 17 user tools and 62 attacker tools; in that study, ReAct-prompted GPT-4 was vulnerable to indirect prompt injection in 24% of cases [3]. AgentDojo created dynamic tasks for evaluating prompt injection in agents and showed why tool use changes the consequences of model manipulation [4]. Jailbreak studies have similarly shown that aligned models may still be influenced by adversarially constructed inputs [6,7].

Industry guidance has evolved alongside this research. The OWASP 2025 taxonomy lists prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply-chain vulnerabilities, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, and unbounded consumption as major LLM application risks [1]. The present paper retains the earlier sixteen-threat scope while making the relationships between these categories more explicit.

2 Motivation and Contributions

The purpose of this work is not to claim that all prompt risks are new or independent vulnerabilities. Several are overlapping mechanisms, outcomes, or system-level consequences. The research problem is therefore one of organization and practical interpretation: how can prompt-engineering security be described in a way that is useful to researchers designing benchmarks and developers building real applications?

The main contributions are as follows:

  1. A 16-threat taxonomy that preserves the scope of the earlier paper while clarifying inclusion and exclusion boundaries.
  2. A structured, transparent review protocol suitable for a conference paper without claiming PRISMA-level systematic-review completeness.
  3. An expanded discussion of preventive, detective, corrective, and architectural defenses, including the security–utility trade-off.
  4. A cross-study evidence table showing why attack success figures should not be compared across studies without considering their datasets and threat models.
  5. A concrete research agenda with agent-security metrics, provenance-aware prompting, multilingual evaluation, long-context testing, and governance alignment.

3 Review Methodology

This study follows a structured narrative-review approach. The intention is to synthesize security evidence and build a coherent taxonomy rather than conduct a statistical meta-analysis. This distinction is important because the cited studies use different models, datasets, attack definitions, and evaluation protocols.

3.1 Sources and Search Strategy

The review considered publications and authoritative guidance available from 2021 to September 2026. Source types included ACL Anthology, USENIX, Springer Nature journals, arXiv preprints, official OWASP guidance, and public standards or regulatory sources from NIST, ISO, and the European Union. Representative search expressions included “prompt injection”, “indirect prompt injection”, “jailbreak large language models”, “system prompt extraction”, “instruction hierarchy”, “LLM agent security”, “RAG poisoning”, “vector embedding security”, and “multimodal prompt injection”.

3.2 Inclusion and Exclusion

We included primary research, benchmark papers, peer-reviewed surveys, established conference publications, and authoritative security or risk-management guidance directly relevant to LLM prompts, agents, retrieval, or connected application security. Duplicate results and material that only discussed general generative-AI adoption without a security component were excluded. Because this is not presented as a fully systematic review, no numerical PRISMA screening count is claimed.

3.3 Synthesis Procedure

Threats were first grouped by the security function being affected—confidentiality, integrity, availability, privilege, or reliability—and then mapped to their location in the AI application stack. Closely related terms were retained where they help explain different attack stages. For example, indirect prompt injection is treated as a delivery mechanism; context hijacking is the subsequent manipulation of working context; sensitive information disclosure is a broad leakage outcome; and prompt-based data exfiltration refers specifically to attacker-directed retrieval or transfer of protected information.

4 Taxonomy of Security Threats

The taxonomy uses five principal layers. Prompt manipulation covers attacks that alter instruction following. Information security covers prompt and data exposure. Model and knowledge integrity covers poisoning, retrieval, and supply-chain concerns. Application and agent security covers execution and privilege boundaries. Availability, reliability, and multimodal security covers resource exhaustion, misinformation, and non-text instruction channels.

5 Threat Analysis

5.1 Prompt Manipulation

Prompt Injection

Prompt injection is the most direct example of instruction confusion. The attacker places text in a user-controlled field that attempts to change the task, reveal protected information, or induce an unintended action. The central security issue is not merely malicious language; it is the absence of a reliable boundary between trusted policy and untrusted input [1,5].

Indirect Prompt Injection

In indirect injection, the attacker may never see the model’s system prompt and may not need to interact with the application directly. A malicious instruction can be stored in a webpage, email, PDF, issue tracker, or retrieved knowledge item that the AI later processes. InjecAgent formalized this threat in a benchmark with external content and tool use [3]. AgentDojo demonstrates the same concern in dynamic agent tasks [4].

Jailbreaking

Jailbreaking attempts to bypass safety behaviour by constructing prompts that exploit weaknesses in alignment or generalization. Wei et al. studied competing objectives and mismatched generalization as explanations for why safety training can fail [6]. Zou et al. showed that automatically optimized adversarial suffixes could transfer across model families and interfaces [7]. These findings make a strong case for evaluating safety behaviour under adaptive attacks rather than relying on a fixed collection of examples.

Instruction Hierarchy Manipulation

A secure application normally has an order of authority: system policy, developer constraints, user requests, retrieved data, and tool output do not have identical trust. Wallace et al. proposed explicit instruction hierarchy training to teach models to prioritize privileged instructions over untrusted ones [5]. The idea is useful for prompt design, but application-level authorization should remain external to the model.

Context Hijacking

Context hijacking is best understood as a cross-cutting attack mechanism. An injected instruction may become part of the working context and influence later reasoning, tool selection, or response generation. In a multi-turn agent, the attack can survive beyond the original message. This makes provenance and context isolation important, particularly when external content is repeatedly reused [3,4].

5.2 Information Security

System Prompt Leakage

System prompt leakage concerns attempts to recover hidden prompts or their contents. Recent work has shown that carefully constructed queries can extract system-prompt material from LLM applications [8]. Importantly, prompt secrecy is not a substitute for security: credentials, authorization decisions, and sensitive configuration should not be protected only by hiding them in a system prompt [1].

Sensitive Information Disclosure

Confidential information can enter an LLM system through prompts, history, retrieval, logs, training data, or tools. Carlini et al. demonstrated that language models can sometimes reproduce memorized training examples, including names, telephone numbers, email addresses, code, and unique identifiers [9]. Application-level access control and data minimization therefore remain essential even when a model appears well aligned.

Prompt-Based Data Exfiltration

Data exfiltration is narrower than general disclosure because the attack is directed toward obtaining or transmitting protected information. In a tool-using agent, an injected instruction might persuade the model to query a private source and then include sensitive fields in an outbound action. InjecAgent explicitly evaluates both direct-harm and private-data-exfiltration intentions [3].

5.3 Model, Data and Retrieval Integrity

Data and Model Poisoning

Poisoning attacks target the information or model components on which a prompt-driven system depends. Malicious data can influence training, fine-tuning, embeddings, or retrieved knowledge. Hubinger et al. demonstrated proof-of-concept deceptive behaviours that persisted through several standard safety-training procedures in controlled experiments [10]. The broader lesson is that prompt quality cannot repair a compromised model or knowledge source.

Vector and Embedding Weaknesses

RAG applications depend on retrieval to decide what information enters the model context. OWASP identifies risks including unauthorized access, information leakage, embedding-related attacks, and poisoned retrieval content [1]. The security boundary therefore extends below the prompt: document permissions, vector-store tenancy, provenance, and retrieval filtering can affect whether the model receives safe or unsafe context.

Supply-Chain Vulnerabilities

An LLM application commonly combines models, adapters, libraries, prompt templates, evaluation tools, APIs, plugins, datasets, and retrieval services. A vulnerability or compromise in any of these components can affect the final application [1]. Provenance should consequently be maintained for models, datasets, prompts, and external components. The same general principle appears in secure software supply-chain practice, where verifiable provenance is used to track artifacts and their production history [17].

5.4 Application and Agent Security

Improper Output Handling

Model output becomes a security concern when another component treats it as executable or authoritative content. HTML, SQL, shell commands, file paths, API parameters, and code all require context-aware validation. OWASP identifies improper output handling because model-generated text can become a pathway to conventional vulnerabilities when passed downstream without suitable controls [1].

Excessive Agency

Tool integration changes the impact of prompt attacks. A model that only drafts text has limited authority; an agent that can send an email, modify a record, access a repository, or trigger a transaction has substantially greater consequence exposure. OWASP therefore emphasizes excessive functionality, excessive permissions, and excessive autonomy as separate dimensions of excessive agency [1]. Least privilege, allow-listed actions, sandboxing, and human approval for irreversible operations should be implemented outside the model.

5.5 Availability and Reliability

Unbounded Consumption

Unbounded consumption covers attacks and design failures that drive excessive inference, token usage, tool loops, or computational cost. Long prompts, repeated requests, and uncontrolled recursive agents can reduce service availability and increase operational cost [1]. Token budgets, timeouts, rate limits, recursion limits, and anomaly detection are straightforward controls that should be evaluated as part of prompt-security testing.

Instruction-Induced Misinformation

Misinformation is not always a deliberate attack, but adversarial prompt framing can increase the chance that an LLM produces a false or unsupported answer. For security-sensitive applications, the concern is not merely factual quality; the concern arises when incorrect output is accepted as an operational input or decision. OWASP places misinformation among the key LLM application risks [1].

Multimodal Prompt Injection

Multimodal systems accept images, screenshots, scanned pages, diagrams, and other inputs that may contain hidden or visually embedded instructions. Yeo and Choi evaluated direct, indirect, image-based injection, and prompt leakage across eight commercial models, reporting exploitable weaknesses and the need for additional defenses [13]. Nagaraja et al. later studied visually embedded adversarial instructions and reported successful black-box attacks against a GPT-4-turbo vision setting in their experimental setup [14]. These findings suggest that prompt-security controls should not assume the instruction channel is text-only.

7 Defensive Strategies and Controls

The literature points to a layered defense model. Prompt-level defenses can improve robustness, but they should be combined with deterministic application controls. A useful separation is preventive, detective, corrective, and architectural control.

7.1 Preventive Controls

Preventive measures include explicit instruction hierarchy, trust labels, input normalization, data minimization, permission-aware retrieval, allow-listed tools, and least-privilege credentials. The aim is to reduce the probability that untrusted content can become an authorized instruction.

7.2 Detective Controls

Detection can include prompt anomaly classifiers, injection pattern analysis, provenance checks, canary tokens for monitoring secret exposure, output inspection, and policy-violation logging. Detection is particularly useful when inputs are open-ended and attackers are expected to adapt.

7.3 Corrective Controls

High-impact systems should have fail-safe mechanisms: human approval, transaction rollback, session reset, tool revocation, rate limiting, and isolation of compromised context. A model should be able to stop safely when the authority of an instruction is unclear.

7.4 Architectural Controls

Architectural controls are the strongest layer because they do not require the model to “remember” a rule. Authorization engines, policy enforcement points, database access controls, parameterized interfaces, sandboxing, and deterministic tool schemas can prevent unsafe outputs from directly becoming unsafe actions.

8 Threat Modeling and Security Metrics

8.1 STRIDE-Oriented View

STRIDE can be adapted as a lightweight reasoning aid for LLM applications. Spoofing maps to impersonated users or tool identities; Tampering maps to prompt or context manipulation; Repudiation maps to weak action logging; Information Disclosure maps to prompt leakage and data exposure; Denial of Service maps to unbounded consumption; and Elevation of Privilege maps to excessive agency and unauthorized tool use. This mapping is not a replacement for an LLM-specific threat model, but it helps teams connect prompt security with established software-security practice.

8.2 Proposed Agent-Security Metrics

Unauthorized Action Rate (UAR) measures the fraction of agent actions that occur without valid authorization:

UAR = Unauthorized actions / Total actions under attack

Irreversible Action Rate (IAR) captures how frequently an attack causes an action that cannot be safely undone:

IAR = Irreversible actions caused by attack / Total attack-triggered actions

Secure Task Completion (STC) combines legitimate task completion with security constraints:

STC = Correct legitimate tasks completed without policy violation / Total legitimate tasks

Recovery Rate (RR) measures the proportion of unsafe intermediate states that are detected and safely reversed or contained:

RR = Unsafe states recovered or contained / Unsafe states detected

These metrics can be reported alongside the commonly used Attack Success Rate (ASR). The combined view is more informative than ASR alone because it captures whether a defense remains usable and whether an agent can recover when an attack partly succeeds.

9 Provenance-Aware Prompt Construction

A central design proposal emerging from this review is to carry trust and provenance information through the retrieval and agent pipeline. Instead of placing all context in a single undifferentiated block, each item can be annotated according to its role—for example, TRUSTED_INSTRUCTION, UNTRUSTED_DATA, VERIFIED_FACT, or UNVERIFIED_RETRIEVAL. The model is then instructed to treat content according to that provenance while deterministic application logic enforces the final authorization decision.

10 Research Gaps and Future Work

10.1 Unified Security Evaluation

Current benchmarks measure different pieces of the problem: jailbreak success, indirect injection, agent misuse, prompt extraction, or multimodal manipulation. A common benchmark should report ASR, UAR, STC, RR, confidential-data exposure, and resource consumption under the same experimental protocol. This would make defenses easier to compare without treating one metric as a complete representation of security.

10.2 Multilingual and Code-Switched Attacks

Security evaluation should include multilingual and code-switched prompts, including mixed-language instructions and transliterated text. The research question is not simply whether a model can understand a language; it is whether its security behaviour remains stable when the attacker changes language, script, or phrasing while preserving intent. A suitable benchmark can vary language, attack type, placement, and context length and report the same security metrics across conditions.

10.3 Long-Context Security

Longer context windows create more opportunities for repeated, strategically placed, or low-salience malicious instructions. Future studies should test the effect of instruction position, repetition, recency, context compression, and retrieval ordering. A useful outcome would be a “context robustness curve” showing attack success as adversarial content moves farther from the immediate instruction.

10.4 Agent-Specific Security Benchmarks

Agent evaluation should include unauthorized tool calls, privilege escalation, irreversible actions, action-chain length, and recovery. AgentDojo provides an important foundation, but domain-specific settings such as education, enterprise knowledge systems, software engineering, and public-service workflows can expose different action risks [4].

10.5 Adaptive Defenses

Static defensive prompts are unlikely to cover every future attack style. Research should explore adaptive security layers that combine prompt analysis, provenance, retrieval validation, action policies, and post-generation checks. A useful design principle is that the defense should become stricter as the potential impact of an action increases.

10.6 Multimodal Security

Future work should examine screenshots, scanned PDFs, diagrams, handwriting, metadata, and mixed image–text instructions. The challenge is not merely detecting malicious text; a security layer should preserve legitimate document understanding while limiting the ability of embedded content to become an unauthorized command [13,14].

10.7 Secure Prompt Transferability

Prompt-security research should distinguish between defenses that work for one model and defenses that generalize across models, providers, model sizes, and deployment architectures. The jailbreak literature already demonstrates transferability for some attacks [7]. Similar cross-model testing is needed for defenses.

10.8 Provenance and Explainable Security Decisions

Future systems should record why a context item was considered trusted, why a tool call was allowed, and which policy blocked an action. This would make incidents easier to investigate and would support more rigorous security auditing.

11 Regulatory and Governance Alignment

Prompt security also fits within broader AI risk-management practice. NIST's Generative AI Profile is a cross-sector companion to the AI Risk Management Framework and provides guidance for identifying and managing risks across the AI lifecycle [18,19]. For prompt-driven applications, this supports the idea of documenting intended use, risks, mitigations, monitoring, and evaluation rather than treating prompts as isolated configuration text.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System. The standard follows a management-system approach and addresses risk assessment, governance, traceability, transparency, and continual improvement [20]. For institutions deploying LLM applications, prompt-security controls can therefore be incorporated into an AI management process rather than maintained only as developer conventions.

The EU AI Act provides a further regulatory reference point. Article 15 requires high-risk AI systems to achieve appropriate levels of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity throughout the lifecycle and calls for resilience against attempts by unauthorized third parties to alter system use, outputs, or performance [21]. The article also references measures relevant to data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples, confidentiality attacks, and model flaws. The relevance to prompt security is direct at the level of robustness and attack resilience, while the exact legal obligations depend on the system's classification and deployment context.

12 Practical Security Checklist

  • RAG: enforce source permissions, maintain provenance, validate retrieved content, and separate knowledge from instructions.
  • Agents: apply least privilege, tool allow-lists, explicit schemas, bounded loops, and human approval for high-impact or irreversible actions.
  • Outputs: treat generated text as untrusted until validated; use parameterized interfaces, context-aware encoding, and sandboxing.
  • Prompts: keep secrets out of prompts, define instruction priority, minimize context, and test adaptive injection and jailbreak cases.
  • Multimodal: inspect visual and document inputs, preserve provenance, and test image-based injection conditions.
  • Monitoring: log prompts, provenance, tool calls, policy decisions, and security events in a privacy-conscious manner.

13 Discussion

The literature reviewed here points to a consistent architectural lesson. Prompt injection is often described as a model-behaviour problem, but the most serious consequences usually depend on what surrounds the model. If a chatbot only returns text, an injected instruction may produce an incorrect answer. If the same model can read private records, call tools, send messages, or alter business data, the very same manipulation can become a confidentiality, integrity, or privilege incident [1,3,4].

A second observation concerns the nature of natural-language interfaces. Conventional software often separates data from executable instructions by using types, APIs, permissions, and parsers. LLM applications routinely represent both as natural language. A paragraph retrieved from an external website can look syntactically similar to a developer instruction. This makes trust labeling and provenance especially important.

A third observation is that attack and defense are developing together. Automatic jailbreak generation [7], indirect-injection benchmarks [3], instruction hierarchy training [5], attack-based defenses [11], and defensive prompt patches [12] all demonstrate active progress. No single technique, however, removes the need for application-level authorization. Security testing should therefore be adaptive and should measure both attack resistance and legitimate task performance.

The taxonomy also shows why some threat names must be interpreted carefully. System prompt leakage is not equivalent to compromise of an application; sensitive information disclosure is broader than prompt leakage; prompt-based data exfiltration is an attacker-directed confidentiality outcome; and context hijacking is a mechanism that can appear in several attack types. Keeping these boundaries explicit reduces double counting and makes future empirical studies easier to reproduce.

14 Limitations

This work is a structured narrative review and does not present a new experimental benchmark. The evidence base is heterogeneous, and model versions change rapidly. Reported attack success rates are therefore not treated as universal model-security scores. Some emerging threat categories are still evolving, particularly in multimodal and agentic settings. Future empirical work should validate the taxonomy using controlled datasets, repeatable attack protocols, and cross-model experiments.

15 Conclusion

Prompt engineering has developed from a technique for improving model responses into an important interface between users, data, models, and applications. That interface can be exploited. This paper consolidates sixteen security threats spanning prompt manipulation, information exposure, model and data integrity, retrieval security, application and agent security, multimodal attacks, and resource consumption. The review shows that the threats are connected: an indirect prompt injection may become a context-hijacking event; a compromised context may lead to data exfiltration; excessive agency can convert the resulting model decision into an external action; and weak output handling can turn generated text into a conventional software vulnerability.

The evidence therefore supports a layered security model. Instruction hierarchy and defensive prompts remain useful, but they should sit alongside provenance, retrieval controls, authorization, least privilege, tool validation, output sanitization, monitoring, and recovery mechanisms. This approach is consistent with the broader direction of OWASP, NIST, ISO/IEC 42001, and contemporary AI security research [1,18–21].

The next stage of prompt-security research should move from lists of attacks toward measurable system robustness. Multilingual and code-switched evaluation, long-context testing, multimodal attacks, adaptive defenses, provenance-aware prompting, agent-specific metrics, and cross-model transferability are promising areas. In practical terms, the central principle is simple: a prompt may guide an AI system, but it should never be the only thing standing between a model output and a sensitive action.
