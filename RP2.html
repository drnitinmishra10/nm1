1. Executive Summary

FairDataGuard proposes an adaptive, data-centric framework for identifying, quantifying, explaining and mitigating multiple forms of bias in machine-learning datasets before they become embedded in downstream models.

The proposal responds to a practical weakness in many machine-learning workflows: fairness is frequently evaluated after model training, while upstream deficiencies such as under-representation, class imbalance, sampling differences, label inconsistency, distribution shift, intersectional sparsity and image/data-quality disparity may already be present in the dataset. The project therefore treats the dataset as a first-class object of Responsible AI assurance.

The principal technical contribution is a proposed Multi-Dimensional Bias Risk Index (MBRI). Instead of treating one indicator as a complete description of dataset bias, FairDataGuard will normalise a vector of bias indicators, investigate their stability and contribution, and produce a traceable aggregate risk profile. This index will not be presented as a universal truth measure; its construct validity and operating thresholds will be evaluated empirically.

A second contribution is an adaptive mitigation engine that maps observed bias profiles to a constrained set of candidate interventions—including sampling, weighting, augmentation, synthetic data generation and bias-aware preprocessing—then evaluates candidates using a common protocol. Candidate selection will be framed as a multi-objective problem involving fairness, predictive performance and robustness.

Core proposition: the project will connect dataset evidence → quantified risk → intervention choice → downstream model validation in one auditable research workflow.

The project is designed for a 12-month duration and a proposed budget of ₹5,00,000. It will use heterogeneous benchmark datasets, with a particular emphasis on healthcare and dermatological imaging as a high-impact validation track, while keeping all claims within the boundaries of publicly available/licensed evidence and experimental validation.

2. Abstract

Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning are increasingly used in healthcare, finance, education, recruitment, public services and other high-impact applications. In such settings, predictive accuracy alone is insufficient to establish trustworthy performance. A model can achieve strong aggregate metrics while exhibiting materially different error rates or predictive quality across subgroups when the underlying training data are imbalanced, under-representative, inconsistently labelled or affected by distributional and acquisition-related differences.

This project proposes FairDataGuard, an adaptive data-centric framework for dataset auditing, bias quantification, mitigation and downstream validation. The framework will investigate seven bias dimensions: class imbalance, representation bias, sampling bias, label bias, distributional bias, intersectional bias, and image/data-quality bias. These indicators will be mapped to a common risk scale and combined into a proposed Multi-Dimensional Bias Risk Index (MBRI). The index will be evaluated for stability, sensitivity, interpretability and construct validity rather than assumed to be meaningful a priori.

The project will then implement an adaptive mitigation mechanism that selects candidate interventions according to the detected bias profile and evaluates them under a fixed experimental protocol. Fairness, predictive performance and robustness will be treated as competing objectives, with Pareto-based analysis used to characterise trade-offs rather than collapsing all outcomes into one arbitrary score.

Validation will span tabular and image datasets, including candidate dermatological datasets with relevant subgroup metadata. Experiments will use held-out testing, repeated runs, bootstrap confidence intervals, effect sizes and appropriate multiple-comparison control. The planned outputs are a validated auditing methodology, MBRI specification, adaptive mitigation engine, reproducible benchmark protocol, software prototype, research publications and potential intellectual property subject to novelty assessment.

Keywords: Responsible AI; Fair Machine Learning; Data-Centric AI; Dataset Bias; Bias Auditing; Bias Quantification; Bias Mitigation; Intersectional Fairness; Healthcare AI; Dermatology AI; Trustworthy AI.

3. Background and Rationale

Bias in AI is not restricted to model architecture. Bias can enter at data collection, sampling, annotation, feature construction, training, evaluation and deployment stages. NIST's SP 1270 explicitly frames bias identification and management as a socio-technical problem across the AI lifecycle. citeturn179321search8turn179321search9

The fairness literature also demonstrates that multiple mathematical definitions of fairness exist and can encode different objectives. Equal opportunity, for example, focuses on comparable true-positive performance across specified groups, while contemporary toolkits such as Fairlearn support both assessment and mitigation using several fairness definitions. citeturn179321search2turn163618search3turn163618search13

The proposal's data-centric emphasis is especially relevant in dermatological AI. A scoping review identified underreporting and underrepresentation of diverse skin types in machine-learning research for skin-cancer detection, making representation-aware evaluation an important research setting. citeturn179321search0

FairDataGuard therefore does not attempt to invent fairness metrics from scratch. Instead, it investigates whether heterogeneous dataset-level evidence can be organised into a validated risk-assessment and mitigation-selection workflow that is transparent, reproducible and useful to dataset curators and model developers.

Research stance: statistical disparity is treated as evidence requiring contextual interpretation—not as automatic proof of discrimination or harmful intent.

4. Problem Statement

Current machine-learning workflows frequently move from data collection and preprocessing to model training, with fairness checks applied later or through separate tools. This creates five practical problems:

  1. Fragmentation: different bias dimensions may be inspected independently, preventing a common dataset-level risk picture.
  2. Weak traceability: raw indicators do not directly explain how a dataset's risk profile should influence mitigation choice.
  3. Manual intervention selection: practitioners may choose oversampling, weighting or augmentation without a systematic rule linked to observed data characteristics.
  4. Trade-off blindness: a mitigation method may reduce a disparity while degrading overall or subgroup performance elsewhere.
  5. Intersectional invisibility: groups defined by combinations of attributes can remain underrepresented even when individual attributes appear acceptable in isolation.
Central research problem: How can an intelligent, reproducible framework identify, quantify and explain multiple dataset-bias dimensions, select appropriate data-level interventions, and validate their effects on fairness, predictive performance and robustness?

6. Research Hypotheses and Questions

6.1 Hypotheses

H1: A multi-dimensional dataset audit provides more informative risk characterisation than any single bias indicator considered independently.

H2: A validated MBRI is positively associated with downstream subgroup disparity under controlled modelling conditions.

H3: Bias-profile-conditioned mitigation selection can achieve comparable or better fairness–utility trade-offs than a single fixed mitigation strategy across heterogeneous datasets.

H4: Explicit robustness evaluation identifies mitigation choices that appear effective under standard test data but are unstable under distributional variation.

6.2 Research Questions

  1. Which measurable dataset indicators provide stable evidence for the proposed bias dimensions?
  2. How should heterogeneous indicators be normalised and combined into MBRI?
  3. How stable are the MBRI weights and rankings under resampling and alternative normalisation choices?
  4. Can the observed bias profile predict which mitigation family is likely to be useful?
  5. What fairness–performance–robustness trade-offs arise for different interventions?
  6. How does FairDataGuard perform across tabular and image datasets and across multiple model families?

10. Multi-Dimensional Bias Risk Index (MBRI)

The original proposal defined MBRI as a weighted sum. The revised research design makes the weighting, normalisation and validation explicit so the index becomes an empirical research object rather than merely a formula. The approach directly addresses the earlier critique that a basic weighted sum needs stronger mathematical and validation detail.

MBRI(D) = Σi=1k wi · zi(Bi(D)) subject to wi ≥ 0,   Σwi = 1,   zi ∈ [0,1]

where D is the dataset, Bi is the raw indicator for bias dimension i, zi is a robust normalisation function, and wi is the dimension weight.

10.1 Normalisation

Primary analysis will use a robust percentile or min–max transformation to map indicators to [0,1]. Because indicators may behave differently under alternative scaling, sensitivity analysis will compare at least two reasonable normalisation schemes.

10.2 Weight learning

Weight estimation will use a constrained, data-driven calibration study on training/validation datasets. Candidate weights will be evaluated on whether the resulting MBRI is stable across resampling and meaningfully associated with downstream subgroup disparities. An expert-weighted sensitivity analysis may be retained as a transparent secondary benchmark, but no subjective weight set will be treated as the unique truth.

10.3 Construct validity

MBRI will be tested against downstream model outcomes. The key question is whether higher dataset risk corresponds, under controlled model settings, to larger subgroup disparities or degraded subgroup robustness. Correlation/regression analysis, bootstrap confidence intervals and ablation studies will be used.

10.4 Stability

Weights and dataset rankings will be recomputed under resampling, alternative normalisation, removal of individual dimensions and plausible metadata missingness. A stable index should not change substantially under small perturbations that preserve the underlying dataset structure.

10.5 Thresholds

Universal labels such as “low”, “medium” and “high” risk will not be fixed arbitrarily. Where thresholding is useful for the prototype, operating points will be calibrated on validation data and reported together with uncertainty and dataset-specific context.

Scientific contribution: the novelty is the empirical construction and validation of a multi-dimensional data-risk index linked to an adaptive intervention workflow—not the claim that a weighted sum is intrinsically novel.

12. Multi-Objective Optimisation

Fairness is not a single universal scalar objective. Fairlearn's documentation notes that fairness is a socio-technical challenge and that different definitions may be relevant in different contexts. citeturn163618search10turn163618search16

FairDataGuard will therefore preserve a vector of objectives:

F — Fairness
Reduce relevant subgroup disparity metrics selected for the application and dataset.
P — Performance
Maintain or improve predictive performance using appropriate task-level measures.
R — Robustness
Maintain stable subgroup and overall performance under distributional variation and repeated experiments.

Candidate solutions will be compared through Pareto dominance. A solution is retained when no other candidate is at least as good on all three objectives and strictly better on at least one. This preserves trade-offs rather than hiding them inside one arbitrary utility score.

Predeclared decision rule

When more than one candidate remains non-dominated, the final choice will follow a documented rule such as: (i) satisfy the minimum acceptable performance constraint; (ii) satisfy minimum subgroup-support/robustness constraints; (iii) minimise the selected fairness disparity; (iv) choose the lower-compute option if outcomes are statistically indistinguishable.

21. Ethical, Privacy and Data-Management Framework

The project follows a Responsible AI approach in which technical metrics are interpreted within application context. Fairness libraries themselves caution that fairness is socio-technical and cannot be solved solely through mathematical metrics. citeturn163618search10turn163618search16

Ethical safeguards

  • Use public or appropriately licensed datasets.
  • Use sensitive attributes only where scientifically justified and permitted.
  • Do not infer sensitive attributes without a documented methodological and ethical basis.
  • Report subgroup coverage and missingness transparently.
  • Separate research validation from clinical deployment.
  • Seek institutional ethics review wherever required.

Data management

  • Maintain dataset provenance, version and licence records.
  • Separate raw data from processed data.
  • Maintain reproducible transformation logs.
  • Apply access controls to sensitive metadata.
  • Minimise retention and remove unnecessary identifiers.
  • Release code and documentation where redistribution of source data is not permitted.
Ethical interpretation: “difference” is not synonymous with “bias.” The project will investigate whether observed differences are related to representation, sampling, labelling, acquisition or other process factors and will document alternative explanations.

25. Sustainability and Scale-Up

After the 12-month grant, the framework can be extended to larger Responsible AI studies, multimodal datasets, NLP/LLM data auditing and cloud-based dataset governance. A sustainable technical architecture will therefore separate the indicator layer, MBRI layer, mitigation library, experiment runner and reporting layer so that new indicators and mitigation methods can be added without redesigning the complete system.

Long-term vision: FairDataGuard can evolve into a general-purpose AI Dataset Audit and Optimization Platform, while the present grant remains focused on a defensible research scope that can be evaluated within 12 months.

27. Anticipated Limitations

  • Not all datasets provide reliable demographic or protected-attribute metadata.
  • No fairness index can encode every social or contextual consideration.
  • Results may differ across domains because fairness definitions and harm models are context dependent.
  • Data-level improvements do not guarantee fair deployment outcomes.
  • Some candidate datasets or mitigation techniques may be excluded because of licence, ethics, compute or metadata constraints.
  • The project focuses on empirical research validation, not clinical or high-stakes deployment.

28. Conclusion

FairDataGuard addresses a central Responsible AI question: how to identify and manage harmful or unintended disparities before they become embedded in downstream machine-learning systems.

The proposal moves beyond a simple bias-detection dashboard by linking heterogeneous dataset evidence to a formally specified and empirically validated MBRI, an adaptive mitigation-selection mechanism, and multi-objective downstream evaluation. The methodological emphasis is deliberately on transparency and testability: every major claim is paired with a measurable indicator, experimental comparison or validation procedure.

The project is scoped for a 12-month research programme with a proposed budget of ₹5,00,000. Its principal outputs—bias-auditing methodology, MBRI, adaptive mitigation engine, benchmark evidence and prototype—can provide a foundation for a larger programme in Fair Machine Learning, Responsible AI, Data-Centric AI and trustworthy healthcare AI.
